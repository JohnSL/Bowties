Signaling UX — Proposal Mockups

A guided path from “I have a signal here” to a configured Signal-LCC, without the user ever seeing an event ID, a CDI path, or a conditional ladder. These mockups deliberately lead with the hard cases — a two-head interlocking mast and a Pennsylvania position-light signal — because the easy case (three-aspect ABS) proves nothing about whether the approach scales. Everything shown is a static mockup; nothing is implemented. Start with the storyboard — it walks the whole setup of the §1 signal, screen by screen, so the later sections have somewhere to hang.

If you are reviewing this, please read this first

The concern we most want tested is whether this approach can express the diversity of real signaling, or whether it only works for textbook examples. Two things make us think it can. First, an aspect decomposes into three independent parts — a name (a label only), a set of up to four lit lamps, and a speed that cascades to the signal behind — so no rulebook is baked in. Second, the harder systems are not excluded: ABS, APB, interlocking and CTC are all in scope (§9). What changes between them is which hardware Bowties allocates and what it generates, and that is our problem, not the user's.

The question we would most like you to try to answer with a counter-example:
Describe a signaling arrangement you actually need — any system, any rulebook, any mast — that this model cannot describe. Not “would be laborious”; cannot describe at all.

Two independent axes, and everything else follows. Method of operation (ABS / APB / CTC) and interlocking are independent. An interlocking can be automatic (e.g. a diamond with no operator), tower-operated, or dispatcher-operated. CTC always implies an interlocking at the control point; the other combinations don't. Where this table comes from — the part worth arguing about.

Every indication in a signal type carries two facts beyond its appearance: the speed it permits past this signal, and the speed it expects at the next one. That is what speed-signaling names already encode — “Medium Clear” means medium here, clear ahead; “Approach” means normal here, stop ahead.

Combine those two facts with the four answers from step 4 and the condition for each indication is mechanical, not clever. Bowties is not inferring intent; it is doing a table lookup the user could do by hand. That is deliberate — a rule the user cannot predict is a rule they cannot debug.

“Adjust…” is the pressure valve. Real railroads have exceptions — a signal held at Stop for a drawbridge, an indication used only during a specific move. Adjust opens the condition for one indication without turning the other five into hand-written logic. We expect most users never to open it, and the ones who do to open it once.

“Add a third head to Rockville” is refused here, with the reason. A third head needs three more drivers and only two remain. Bowties offers the two things that actually help: move the signal to another node, or drop a lamp from the head. It does not offer “try anyway”.
Why five and not one. These ceilings are consumed by different things and exhaust in different orders. A layout heavy on multi-head masts runs out of drivers; one heavy on complex interlocking logic runs out of logic slots; one with long signalled sections runs out of cascade links. A single “capacity” number would hide which decision to revisit.
These are a Signal-LCC's numbers. A Tower LCC+Q counts entirely different things — input and output bits, and characters of program text — so a layout using one for APB or CTC logic (§9) sees a different set of ceilings for the logic while the display ceilings above stay exactly as they are. Capacity is per board, and Bowties has to say which board is full.

6. Bring-up — prove the lamps before you write any logic

This mirrors the sequence experienced users converge on: wiring, then one lamp, then one aspect, then the whole mast, then logic, then cascade. Each stage isolates one class of fault. The tooling that exists today does not guide this order, and most of the pain reported in the community comes from skipping it.

Read it aloud and it is the whole rule. CP Banks is at Stop because its block is occupied. It publishes Stop. Rockville's block is clear but the signal ahead is at Stop, so Rockville shows Approach and publishes Approach in turn. The user never chose an event, an address, or a circuit number.
Two “next signals” because the route diverges. This is the part a per-head model gets wrong. One display, two sources.
Open question for reviewers. Bowties knows about blocks and turnouts, but has no model of track topology — it cannot work out for itself which signal is “ahead”. So the user names it. Is naming the next signal acceptable, or does it become unmanageable on a large layout, where deriving it from a track plan would be the only workable answer?

9. Beyond plain ABS — APB, interlockings, and CTC

This is a proposal about signaling, not about a circuit board. The harder systems are in scope. What changes between them is what Bowties has to model, which hardware it allocates, and what it generates — none of which the user should have to reason about. Two axes, not four peers: method of operation (ABS / APB / CTC), and whether a given location is an interlocking (and if so, who operates it). CTC implies an interlocking at the control point; ABS and APB do not, but can have automatic or tower-operated interlockings dropped into them.

APB — the case that breaks a per-signal model

A train takes the single track between two sidings. Signals behind it follow ordinary ABS rules. Every opposing signal in the whole section is knocked down to Stop and stays there until the train leaves — that is the tumbledown, and it is a property of the section, not of any one signal.

One new idea: the section. Direction cannot belong to a signal, because eight signals share it. So APB introduces exactly one object above the signal — a stretch of single track with a direction that a train takes and releases. The signals themselves are unchanged from §1; they just gain one more input.
And one new hazard: every latch needs a release. A direction that is taken but never released leaves the railroad deadlocked; one released too eagerly makes opposing signals clear up in a train's face. Bowties generates the release alongside the latch and refuses to produce one without the other — this is the documented way people get "fleeting" signals, and it is not something a user should have to remember.

Interlocking — the other axis

Interlocking is a property of the location, not of the method of operation. Any of the three methods above can have interlockings inside them, operated three different ways:

OperatorWhere it appearsBowties additionally modelsBowties generates
Automatic Diamonds, light-traffic junctions, some drawbridges — no human in the loop + turnouts, routes, first-come-first-served and time locking A conditional ladder
Local (tower) Manned interlocking towers + turnouts, routes, operator lever inputs A conditional ladder
Dispatcher (CTC) Every CTC control point + dispatcher levers with route and time locking An STL program
The one implication tying the two axes together. CTC always implies an interlocking at the control point — that's the definition. ABS and APB can each be plain (no interlockings), or can have automatic or tower-operated interlockings dropped into them, e.g. an ABS main crossing a branch at a diamond.
The masts never move. No board in the range other than the Signal-LCC drives lamps or holds aspects, so a signal's display always lives there. For APB and CTC only the logic relocates, and it reaches the masts the same way any other logic does — by sending the aspect events they already listen for. Nothing about §1 through §8 changes.
Generating a program instead of settings is a real shift. For ABS and interlocking Bowties fills in configuration fields. For APB and CTC it writes source code into the node, which compiles it on board and reports back whether the build succeeded. That gives Bowties something unusual — a compiler it can show results from — and one obligation: if generated code ever fails to build, the user must never be shown a syntax error for a program they did not write.
Honest status. ABS and interlocking are worked through in detail in this document. APB and CTC are worked through at the level shown here — we know what they need, where they run and what we would emit, and not much more. They are deferred, not excluded, and if a reviewer thinks the sketch above is wrong that is exactly the feedback we want before building any of it.

10. What this deliberately cannot do

Stated up front so reviewers can tell us which of these is disqualifying rather than merely inconvenient. Some are hardware limits we cannot lift; others are choices we can revisit.

Display limits — cannot be lifted

These bind wherever the logic runs, because a mast only ever lives on a Signal-LCC.

  • More than 4 lit lamps in one indication.
  • More than 8 indications on a mast, or 16 when two mast slots are chained.
  • More than 16 lamps on one board — roughly four heads.
  • A signal whose lamps span two boards.
  • Silencing a mast's state events. They are always emitted.

Our choices — reviewable

  • No track topology. The user names the next signal; Bowties cannot infer it from a track plan. This is the exclusion we are least comfortable with.
  • Indications are drawn from a fixed 32-name list. A scheme with more than 32 distinct names must reuse or rename them for display.
  • No cab signaling or approach-speed enforcement.
  • Signals are configured one at a time. There is no “signal this whole subdivision” bulk operation, though nothing prevents one later.
Not on either list: APB, CTC, route locking and held signals. All four need latched state and, in two cases, an object above the signal. That makes them later and it makes them run on different hardware — see §9 — but none of them is ruled out by the model in this document.
The one we are least sure about. Naming the next signal by hand (§7) is fine for twenty signals. We do not know whether it stays fine for two hundred, or whether at some point only a track plan will do. If you have a large signalled layout, this is the question we would most like you to answer.

The two decisions hiding in this table. First: is signal type a first-class thing users create, name and share — or just a saved template with no independent existence? Second: is the indication / aspect distinction worth carrying into the UI, given the node itself blurs it? Both are cheap to settle now and expensive to change later.
Where these words collide with Bowties today. Bowties already uses facility, channel, role and style. A signal is a facility; a lamp driver is a channel binding. The words above sit on top of those rather than replacing them, but none of signal type, head, indication or aspect exists in the glossary yet.
